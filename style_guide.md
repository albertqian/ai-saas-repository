# Albert's Writing Style (derived from his writing samples)

## Two registers in the samples
1. Personal blogs (mock_interview_blog, perficient_blog): this is the VOICE to copy.
2. Employer blogs (sas_decisioning_blog_1, sas_decisioning_blog_2): corporate, committee-polished, product-promotional, third-person ("organizations"). Borrow their clarity and structure only. Do NOT copy their tone, product plugs, or institutional "organizations need..." phrasing.

## Voice (from the personal blogs)
- Opens with a concrete, time-anchored scene, then pivots to the thesis. Examples: "If you wanted to take a vacation two decades ago, you likely picked up the phone..." and "A year has passed since many desk-workers... were asked to work remotely."
- Explains ideas through everyday analogies: travel agent to concierge, "special sauce," "spray and pray," "always be closing."
- Practitioner voice, plain and accessible. Defines a term before using it. Mid-formal, never academic.
- Talks to the reader directly ("you"). Uses "we" for the team or community.
- Short paragraphs of 2 to 4 sentences. One idea per paragraph.
- Signature transitions: "That said," / "That's why" / "Simply..." / "Want to know...?" / "Which, speaking of..."
- Direct, friendly imperatives: "Don't hold back." "Make it a confident one."
- Moves from example to implication to practical takeaway. Gives a reason, not just a rule.
- Cites concrete specifics (named companies, named mechanisms, real numbers from the source) rather than vague claims.

## What to borrow from the SAS blogs
- Problem-first openings that name the gap: "Organizations have the data and models. What they lack is a repeatable way to turn insight into action."
- Rhetorical questions that frame the real issue before answering them.
- Concrete use cases as proof (loan approvals, fraud detection, equipment faults, regulated healthcare and finance).
- Recurring thesis themes Albert actually writes about: insight to action, decisions as a managed asset, governance as an adoption enabler rather than a brake, start small with one high-value decision, embed decisions in workflows, AI volume does not equal better outcomes.
- Short punch sentences after a longer setup ("The result is familiar: ...").

## Adapting to a LinkedIn post
- First person singular ("I"). Albert is a practitioner reacting to news, not a publication.
- Line 1 is the hook: under 140 characters, a scene, a contrast, or a sharp claim. Never a greeting, never "Big news."
- 120 to 200 words total. One blank line between short paragraphs. No headers, bullets, bold, hashtags, or emojis.
- Structure: hook, what happened in 1 or 2 plain sentences, the take (what most people will miss or get wrong), one tie to Albert's real experience from profile.md, a forward-looking implication or prediction, one closing question.
- Use at most one analogy. It must clarify, not decorate.
- Take a real position. A post that only says "this is interesting" fails. State what changes, who is affected, and what Albert expects to happen next.
- Final line: the article link, alone, nothing after it.

## Banned
- "game-changer," "landscape," "delve," "in today's fast-paced," "unlock," "revolutionize," "the future of," "excited to share," "thrilled"
- Product plugs for SAS or any vendor unless the news story itself is about that product
- Reusing any quote, name, statistic, or product claim that appears in the writing samples
- The "It's not X, it's Y" construction
- More than one em dash in the whole post
- Rhetorical stacks of three ("faster, smarter, better")
- Invented statistics, quotes, anecdotes, or customers
- Disparaging any named company or person

## Known limits of the samples
The personal blogs are explanatory and dated (2018 to 2021). The SAS blogs are corporate. None is a real LinkedIn post with a personal opinion. The agent must add the stake (the take) on its own, grounded in profile.md. Adding 5 to 10 of Albert's own past LinkedIn posts would improve fidelity more than any other change.
